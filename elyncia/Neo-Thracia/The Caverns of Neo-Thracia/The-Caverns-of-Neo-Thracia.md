The Caverns of Thracia is the first in a series of dungeons and adventure scenarios designed and illustrated in entirety by Jennell Jaquays for use with TSR’s original Dungeons and Dragons® Rules systems. The contents of this adventure may need to be changed by the individual referee to suit the campaign he or she moderates. This product has been approved by TSR for use with Dungeons and Dragons®. ### Designer’s Comments

The Caverns of Thracia has been designed to accommodate adventurers of the 1st and higher levels. There are some segments of the adventure that will be a pushover for low level groups while one or two areas will send the higher level characters ducking for cover if played correctly. As you read through this scenario, and I do recommend reading through it before commencing play, you may notice at times that the designer (self) becomes quite informal. This is intended, since this adventure was not designed to have snob appeal or be the last word in gaming aids. This is now your adventure. I have put down possibilities and probabilities and a few remarks to go with them. It is now up to you to breathe life into the denizens of this scenario. There are no high power artifacts in these caverns and only one creature has very high power magic use. I would surmise that even a group of fighters could survive in this adventure (although they would definitely miss out on some historical aspects and clues) and haul out a goodly sum of loot. Treasure is purposefully rare (although there are some rather nice ones) and the monsters that are aware of each other in this dungeon are usually working together. The only opposing groups in this game are the humans and the beast men and since they don’t frequent the same areas, there is little interaction between them except for constant raiding.

### Starting the Adventure

The best place to start any adventure is in a place of familiarity to the characters. Drop a few hints and let the players take it from there asking pertinent questions of the locals and being generally nosey, a pre-requisite for all adventurers. To speed things up, the referee might want to use the section titled, *The Taverns of Thracia*, to randomly assign knowledge to characters.

### Notes for the Judge

A\) This adventure is not specifically located on the Judges Guild world. It can be, but it is not. Suggested areas for its location would include: A large, misted, off-shore island, possibly once connected via landbridge to the mainland; A ruined city near the edge of a subtropical forest; A ruined city on the edge of a mountain or in large, forsaken hills.

B\) I have tried something different in the numbering system used. Instead of renumbering each time a level is changed, I have simply continued with consecutive numbers. Thus, instead of Level 2, Room [23](#23---the-sacrificial-altar-of-the-dark-one), it is merely Room [50](#50---a-converted-crypt).

C\) Some rooms may have to be beefed up or toned down and some of their contents changed altogether if they do not match your world.

D\) There are several monsters in this adventure that were created especially for the scenarios presented. They are not to be taken as official D&D monsters, but instead are just local color to foil the rule book memorizers in an adventuring party. Nothing seems to scare someone who knows the rules like something totally unknown.

E\) A complete table of random encounters for each level is provided with the adventure. If a wandering monster is indicated, use the chart for that level. If a level does not have a chart, it does not have wandering monsters.

F\) Due to the size of this adventure, it is doubtful that it can be played in one sitting.

G\) In the Caverns of Thracia there exists a religion that may not exist elsewhere. These are the worshippers of Thanatos, the death god or as he is currently called “The Dark One”. Clerics in the service of this lawful evil god assume a lawful evil alignment although actual followers need not be of any particular alignment, since death can work through anything. The clerics in the service of this god speak and read in the Thracian language (but not the very ancient language, see history for details). They have the following special abilities: At 4th level they may animate dead as per the spell once per day; at 6th level they may speak with dead as per the spell once per day; at 7th level they may use finger of death once per day as per the spell. These spells are not in addition or extra to their normal number of clerical spells. In addition to the above, the cleric in the service of death may control a number of undead equal to those turned by a good cleric. A cleric may never control more undead than the indicated maximum number of beings that are turnable (2d6 or maximum of 12). A “D” on the chart indicates instant control. A number indicates that the number must be rolled over on 2d8; a “T” indicates control of 1d6 of the undead creatures. Once these creatures are under the control of a cleric of Thanatos, they will obey his or her command until they are destroyed. Undead of 5+ hit dice may make a saving roll once per day of equal to or over the controlling cleric’s wisdom +2.

H\) Have fun and enjoy yourselves. As real as gaming may seem at times, it’s still only a game. Direct any questions, complaints, hate mail, etc. to Jennell Jaquays c/o Judges Guild, 1165 N. University Ave., Decatur, IL 62526. If a question or topic is pertinent, the answer may be published in one or both of the magazines put out by Judges Guild.

# The Caverns of Neo-Thracia

### A Short History

This history is not provided to give the players a background on the history of the caverns. As far as the players are concerned, the caverns are a part of a lost civilization(s) that disappeared centuries before. Little is known of them except, possibly, its location and a few wild stories (see *The Taverns of Thracia*). Instead, this history is provided to give the referee a historical index of various events that occurred in the development of the cavern societies. It will also include an overview of the cultural development of the early Thracian civilizations.

Before man existed, or possibly before he discovered the benefits and curses of civilization, there existed the reptile race. The reptile race, while not large, had its own centers of civilization, including large cities and underground temple complexes. As man waxed, the reptile race waned, losing much of its arcane civilization and slowly degenerating or regressing into the race of beings that men call “lizard men”. A few pockets of more advanced lizard men exist, but they are slowly dwindling as the race dies out. Long before man discovered the caverns, the reptile race was beginning to slip backwards. The Immortal King, the ruler of the tattered remnants of lizard men who still know the ways of sorcery, was near death and had been dying for several centuries. With the coming of man to the area (a new source of food and slaves for the still powerful reptiles) came the death of the Immortal King. But his was not a true death. The dead body remained animated by the creature’s spirit. Even in death the Immortal King continued to rule as a lich, entombed in the ancient underground temple. Slowly the reptile race regressed to their present state, but not before they had watched the now plentiful humans discover the caverns and build their own temple and burial complexes. The lizard men at last sealed up the various entrances to their now crumbling great hall and slunk off into the fetid swamps, to return later as mercenaries for the beast kings.

Meanwhile, the humans had discovered the caverns, not realizing that the once feared reptile men (they were merely legendary now and the Immortal King was a bogeyman to scare children) still occupied hidden portions of the caverns. At this point the men were beginning to develop a civilization comparable to the early Mycenaean or Cretan civilizations of this world’s past. The art and architecture were almost identical with the above mentioned cultures. Eventually they sealed off several of their tomb complexes and began to concentrate on the civilization above ground. Half a millennium later they would construct the temples on second level and 200 years after that they would begin the golden age of Thracian culture.

The actual golden age began with the discovery of an immense cavern, deep below the natural caverns. It was not a natural chamber, but no note was taken of that. About this same time, the Thracian people had begun to use sub-human creatures for slaves, such as the gnolls, minotaurs and dog brothers, who began to carve the underground caverns into a huge garden and palace. During the reign of Argos II the vast work was completed and the underground area became a playground for the Thracian royalty. However, during the reign of Agamemnos, Argos’ grandson, the huge number of beast men that laired in the caverns revolted and took over the palace, wounding the king to the death and causing his gentry to flee. Most never made it to the surface. Those who did inspired either fear or fierceness into persons hearing the tale. The few bands of vengeful citizens that entered the caverns were never seen again.

Thus, did the beast men set themselves up in the underground domain, retaining control of the entire 3rd, 4th and 2nd levels while allowing the 1st level to go untouched. They chose an intelligent minotaur to rule them and then set down to decide what to do with their numerous captives.

Some 150 years later the worship of Thanatos, the death god, was outlawed and the few priests who served death were either tortured to death or were sealed in the chapel of Thanatos on the 1st level.

The first wave of invaders came soon after. On the Sesquicentennial anniversary (150) years of the overthrow of Thanatos, an invading army of barbarians (all non-Thracians are barbarians) was spurred on by descendants of the death worshippers to utterly destroy the city. The lax Thracian army was no match for frenzied barbarians and all but a fraction of the city’s population were put to the sword or carried off into slavery. During the destruction, the entrances to the caverns were destroyed and covered with rubble.

The few remaining Thracians attempted to make some kind of reconstruction but less than a quarter century later a second invasion of vicious invaders struck again. Those Thracians who fled into the encroaching jungle survived while the rest died. The few who did escape slipped back into savagery. 200 years later, they rediscovered the caverns and with them they found not only a new/old religion in the worship of the god known only through legend as “The Dark One”, but also the beast men.

The beast men by this time had taken over the entire cavern complex (those parts they could easily find without looking hard) and had opened up the caverns to the surface again. With the coming of humans again, they saw entirely new possibilities for food and pleasure. Soon after the caverns were reopened, the reptile race once again sought the location of their legends and in doing so allied themselves with the beast men.

The caverns are now the holy places both for the descendants of the Thracians and for the once powerful reptile race whose degenerated offspring still seek the reason for their existence.

### Some Notes About The Thracian Language

The very ancient Thracians had a written language. There are a few instances where it can be found in the caverns. However, between the time of the sealing of the Ancient Thracian tombs and the beginning of the golden age, the Thracian language changed drastically (something like the difference between classical Latin and modern Italian). A character who can read the Thracian language may pick out one word in ten of the Ancient Thracian language.

## The Taverns of Neo-Thracia (Rumors)
----------------------

You will hear it before you ever see the place. In the lake-towns and the fossil-road inns, in the reed-hush where the barge-folk drink, the Caverns live as talk --- scraps of legend, half a truth wearing the other half's coat. None of it arrives whole. A lost thing stays lost not by silence, but by a hundred tellings that no longer agree.

Use this section or leave it; the referee decides. It hands out **legends** as *data shards*, splinters of old knowing that drifted loose from wherever they started and picked up grit on the way. Scatter them among your NPCs, or, when the table wants momentum more than mystery, deal them straight to the players by the method below.

Each shard a character carries, they carry on their own (slip of paper or private character sheet note). Tell them they *know* this thing about the road ahead. Tell them they may keep it close, trade it, or lie about it as they please --- a shard withheld becomes its own kind of coin. And tell them nothing of its weight, because not every shard rings true. The false ones wear an **(F)** where only the referee can see it. The referee has no obligation to inform the player as to the veracity of this information.

### Adventurers' Guild Referee Intel

*The following circulate among Ainos locals, river traders, and hill-tribe contacts. Veracity unconfirmed. Sources include 489 years of oral drift, deliberate misdirection, and genuine memory. Some may prove false --- marked where strong suspicion exists. Confidence rates how likely a claim holds true given what the Guild could corroborate, scored X/20 --- never how loudly the tavern repeats it.*

**1.** (F) *Someone in Ainos pays well for intact Thracian historical artifacts --- 200 to 2,000 cash per item, no questions asked. The buyer stays unnamed.* **12/20** The price keys to *pattern-intact*, not age: an unbroken inscription, a live glyph-matrix, a Named tool the Iron Curse spared. Double rumored for anything holding a working daemon-binding. The "no questions" clause builds the market; the unnamed patron asks questions of their own, elsewhere. The referee chooses privately who stands behind the broker --- a Delian cataloguer, a Dominion procurement cell, an Amaranth relic-claim agent --- and every sale feeds *someone's* picture of the Caverns, possibly the party's own employer, possibly a rival. First sale opens the relationship; third sale opens the surveillance.

**2.** *The Beast Folk began as loyal Imperial citizens, twisted by a curse when the old spirit-networks failed.* **4/20 --- likely false** The account surfaces only among imperial-descended households, and it does two jobs for them: it erases the enslaved labor that carved the palace, and it reframes a liberation-kingdom as a malfunction awaiting repair. The low score encodes the slant, not mere doubt. The teller never knows the deeper joke --- in this world all human folk carry the fae braid, so the "pure" category the curse-story tries to restore never held in the first place. A character repeating this to a beast-kin patrol or an Amaranth scout marks themselves remnant-aligned, with reaction consequences the referee should not soften.

**3.** *Beware the Yellow Death.* **14/20** Repeated by independent sources across two decades; no teller will specify. The vagueness carries the weight --- the warning fits yellow spore-blooms in the deep karst, a sulfurous cast to pooled necro-data fog, orichalcum gone jaundiced from feeding on something wrong, and at least one tragic mix-up with a hireling's bright-yellow pilgrim poncho. A local asked "yellow *what*?" gives a look, not an answer, because locally everyone already lost someone to a yellow thing. The referee keeps the referent open and lets the player's dread assign it.

**4.** *Don't trust the trees.* **?** The warning runs triple. Canopy ambushers --- gnoll patrols and tribesmen --- fight from the branches at the surface entrances. The bound grove-fae deeper down carry a desire-steering charm that works on gnolls as readily as on delvers. And the trees *listen*: word travels the grove, so what a party says near one bound dryad, the whole garden knows by nightfall. Underneath sits the campaign's sharpest faction-lever --- the grove-fae grieve sisters felled for palace boiler-fuel, and a party arriving with water, honest mourning, and a word about the boilers acquires allies no army could buy.

**5.** *The Tribesmen perform human sacrifice in a ceremony tied to the death-cult sanctuary.* **13/20** True in the act, open in the meaning. With Death silent for 489 years and the soul-roads luring spirits into eddies, a death-rite worked at the threshold of the dreaming god reads less like barbarism and more like *maintenance* --- the hill-tribes may run the last funerals in the Nexus that still function. Whether the offering feeds the sleeping god, the restless dead, or something wearing the Dark One's name stays the referee's to set. The word "human" carries the teller's contempt; the rite's purpose carries something the teller never asks after.

**6.** *(Capability, not intel.)* A character has come to read **New Thracian** --- once outright on this expedition, then slowly on a successful Thought test. Ancient Thracian stays nearly shut: one word in ten on a good day. Track this on the character's sheet. The grant opens the human strata; the corrupted glyph-code of the old nodes stays closed to it, and yields only at a fed lararium where a Lorekeeper-aspect can attempt decryption. Whoever draws this becomes the delver the others hand every potsherd to.

**7.** *Beware the God of Death.* **14/20** Locals say *beware*, never "active" --- and the distinction matters. One reed-hush drunk stated the theology exactly without knowing it: *he sleeps, he walks*. Both hold true. The god dreams in a wound under the deep levels, sealed by a rite that never finished; the Incarnation that patrols the upper death-strata reads as a fragment of that dream given watch-duty, killing intruder beast-kin and human alike. The warning cuts every direction --- beware the patrol, beware waking the sleeper, beware that the sleeper may hold the only true account of what happened the year the empire fell, and answers from dreaming gods cost.

**8.** (F) *The rope bridges in and around the Caverns have rotted through; crossings stay unsafe.* **5/20 --- likely stale** The phrasing reads decades old. A crossing rotted out once, word walked to the lake-towns, and the world changed behind the rumor's back --- recent traffic implies at least one bridge has since gone up new and sound. The lie carries a timestamp, not a liar. A party trusting it burns rope and turns rigging safety-lines over a span that holds fine, every wasted turn ticking the wandering-monster clock; a party that reaches fresh hemp instead learns that *someone maintains this crossing, recently and well* --- better intelligence than most of the true reports.

**9.** *A pool somewhere in the ruins grants protection or restoration --- "for safety, seek the Pool of Watery Wonders," exact phrase.* **10/20** The archaic phrasing fossilizes an old instruction. The pool sits at the bottom, inside the beast-king's palace, and offers four workings --- summon, locate, scry, transit --- through a chained activation that reads as a facility console of the imperial era, its teleport drift the mark of 489 years without a calibration daemon alive to correct it. "For safety" once meant *the way out*: an evacuation route the fleeing gentry took during the revolt, retold until the context wore away. True on three readings --- the pool does grant escape, reaching it demands surviving the whole descent, and the moment intruders become known the same console becomes the king's longest arm.

**10.** *A Hall of Laughing Skulls leads somewhere significant; the skulls keep laughing in the dark.* **8/20** The hall reads as a legacy access-corridor --- skulls at uniform height, eye-light, a two-point simultaneous-press lock with an anti-tamper bite, a chuckle on every touch. The laughter plays as a stuck acknowledgment-chime, centuries past its last maintenance; the bite runs as the anti-tamper subroutine still sampling unauthorized hands. Beyond waits a live examiner --- a guardian who watches from the golden light and grants passage on judgment, demanding wit or tribute. The rumor keeps the riches and drops the exam, which gets the unprepared killed by the omission.

**11.** *Be wary of statuary.* **?** The Iron Curse rotted the empire's iron in weeks, but marble and bronze took no harm --- so where the iron automatons seized and died, the stone and bronze guardians kept their footing, defaulting after five centuries to their oldest standing order: *an intruder is an intruder*. The warning makes delvers wary of every carved figure, the inert and the dryad-carved and the console-pillar alike, which taxes torches and turns until complacency meets the one statue that walks. A guardian still running its binding may sometimes answer re-addressing rather than only destruction --- the right password, the right ritual authority, the right offering. The watchmen never got word they stood dismissed; the rumor does not say they cannot get re-hired.

**12.** *Deep beneath the surface: a sunlit garden and a sparkling underground palace; something feeds it mana-flow.* **15/20** The structure matches architectural records from before the revolt --- likely true in form, uncertain in present state. A sustained false-sun still burning under a manufactured cloud-layer reads as a major standing power-draw that has somehow never gone dark, which turns the marvel into a clue: *what still feeds it* points at the lost power-core, a ley-tap, or the wounded node that holds the dreaming god. The beauty and the wound share the same stone --- the garden *is* the pleasure-palace the enslaved carved under a false sun, tended now by captive grove-fae whose dead heat the boilers. The teller hears Eden; the history holds five layered centuries of bondage.

**13.** *An old hermit may dwell near the ruins --- half-fae, reportedly useful; locals treat the figure as historical more than current.* **7/20** A wanderer carrying the threshold-and-road affinity could persist in a lost place by belonging to none of its warring factions --- keeping guest-rite where empires do not reach, possibly near one of the old Ways. Aid offered would run real: a hearth where the wandering-monster clock stops, lore of which thresholds still open and where, and uncorrupted local memory of the *why* the rest of the Nexus forgot. The cost runs real too --- hospitality given accrues hospitality owed, a small errand or a sigil carried, faerie-kindness with a quiet price. Whether the hermit still lives, or the rumor points 200 years stale at a successor, a ghost, or an empty burrow, stays the referee's to decide. The table needs one knock that gets answered kindly.

**14.** *Buried with a dead king lies a powerful but evil sword that never rusted.* **9/20** A blade kept whole through the Iron Curse names itself a Named one --- iron tied to myth or deed deeply enough to slip the rot --- and the naming that preserved it never reads neutral. "Evil" points at the deed the blade stays storied for, still running; the wielder inherits the bargain that kept the edge sharp. *Which* dead king triangulates the campaign: the last imperial king who died in his garden, the deathless reptile-king who shares his tomb and still moves, or a beast-dynasty liberation-king whose grave the word "evil" slanders. Treasure-talk inflates the prize and strips the cost with each telling; the grave keeps both.

**15.** (F) *The caverns go no deeper than two levels; only darkness lies below.* **4/20 --- likely false** The claim matches exactly how far the hill-folk themselves descend --- the horizon of the knower mistaken for the floor of the place, assembled entirely from honest reports. Below the supposed bottom sit the false-sun garden, the palace, the console-pool, the dead king's sword, and the dreaming god, so this single false floor conceals nearly every true prize the briefing names. A party trusting it provisions for two levels and runs out of light, food, and clock three levels down. Dealt alongside the garden-rumor, the contradiction does the work --- *a sunlit garden deep under the earth, yet no deeper than two levels?* --- and the careful player finds the seam.

**16.** (F) *The beast-king hoards immense wealth --- possibly vast stores of working magitech.* **6/20 --- likely false** The same imperial-descended households that tell the curse-story tell this one, and the score marks the slant. The rumor reframes a liberation-kingdom as a thieves' den, legitimizing plunder while erasing that the "wealth" got built by the beast-kin's own enslaved ancestors. The deep level holds power, not a hoard --- the console, the false-sun garden, the boiler-works, sacred objects, a *population*. The lethal misread sends a party fighting *toward* the throne-room when the thing that actually pays runs the other way: parley, with a court that has feared returning plunderers for five centuries and will give a warband exactly the reception that fear earned.

**17.** *A remnant of an ancient pre-human civilization endures deep in the caverns --- older than the Thracians, older than the tomb-builders, possibly still present; it speaks through dreams.* **10/20** The reptile-folk held the karst before the first humans came, dwindled to the lizard-men, and left a deathless sorcerer-king entombed in the ancient hall while his descendants guard the Shrine of the Ancient Ones. He carries the one memory in the Nexus that reaches behind the manufactured category of "human" --- the braid-truth recorded as plain fact before anyone had reason to bury it --- and possibly the only account of who cut the unnatural deep cavern the golden age built its palace atop. A lich among lichdom in an Age of Undeath invites a fear the referee can sustain: the players may suspect a tie to the trapped Imperator, where almost certainly none runs --- the reptile-king predates the whole scheme. What he knows costs like a campaign.

**18.** *The Beast Folk enslave folk taken from the surrounding valleys, worked in darkness and steam.* **12/20** True, and the briefing's hardest entry. The captives run as fae-folk lineages --- though in this world that distinction means less than the teller thinks --- held in the boiler-works that light the garden and heat the console-pool. A kingdom founded by the enslaved, five centuries on, reproduces bondage: the same machine wearing a new operator. Imperial-descended sources tell it with satisfaction to launder their own history; beast-kin dissidents tell it with grief against their own court --- the same true sentence, opposite mouths. The fact holds regardless of who carries it, and it asks the party to hold two things at once: the slander of rumors 2 and 16 stays false, *and* this kingdom does a real wrong now.

**19.** (F) *A good heart, or a good person's touch, lays the restless dead to rest.* **5/20 --- likely false (imported)** The doctrine arrives with Dominion and Free Cities newcomers and may hold true where the soul-roads still run; here it kills the faithful. Undeath in this Nexus reads as a condition of the wounded Pattern, not a stain that virtue scours --- Death fell silent, the passage-rites broke, the dead cannot move on because the *infrastructure* failed, not because evil won. What actually moves the dead runs on correct practice --- the right rite, the right name, the right offering at the right node --- orthopraxy, not a kind heart. The earnest character who carries this confidence into the death-strata pays the bill the grave sets; yet a good character who learns the working rite anyway becomes something better than a banisher --- a ferryman.

**20.** *(Capability, not intel.)* A character speaks **New Thracian** as the hill-tribes hold it --- clear on a successful Thought test, garbled to the Singing Caryatids' tables on a failure. Track this on the character's sheet. A phrasebook copied in New Delos carries *pre-Maelstrom* New Thracian, while the deep clans speak it as five centuries of isolation reshaped it --- so outsider-learned speech may draw a penalty among the hill-tribes precisely because it sounds antique, the mark of someone taught by the wrong source. Clear speech opens the cheapest route to pinning the Caverns' location and the only living human account of the deep history. Speech grants the *possibility* of the conversation; trust still answers to deeds and offerings.
